Things you have been told that are simply
not true

Plenty of agents and buyers walk away from an incarcerated owner because the paperwork is unfamiliar to them. The law is not the problem. The logistics are, and logistics can be handled.

"You can't sell without being there in person."

You can. Florida deeds are signed in front of a notary and two witnesses. Institutions have notaries, and mobile notaries can be scheduled. I handle the scheduling.

"You have to wait until you get out."

You don't, and waiting usually costs the most. Taxes, insurance, code fines and a vacant house all keep running. A sale stops the bleeding now.

"A power of attorney from a prisoner isn't valid."

It is, when it is done right: signed before two witnesses and a notary, limited to this one property, and reviewed by the title company before closing.

"Your family will have to handle it without you."

No. Family can help, but the owner stays in control. Nobody can sell the owner's property without the owner's signature or a power of attorney the owner chose to give.

Straight answers

The questions families ask me first. If yours is not here, call. Asking costs nothing.

Is this legal?+

Yes. An incarcerated person keeps the right to own and sell property. Florida's rule against inmates running a business specifically allows them to communicate as needed to protect property they owned when they were committed. Selling a house you already own is exactly that. I am not a lawyer, and I encourage every owner to have one look at the paperwork, but there is nothing unusual or improper about this sale.

Does the owner need a power of attorney?+

Not always. There are two ways to sign. The owner can sign the deed personally at the institution in front of a notary and two witnesses. Or the owner can sign a limited power of attorney naming one trusted person to sign for this property only. In Florida a power of attorney has to be signed before two witnesses and a notary to be valid. Either way, the title company reviews the documents before closing. I will tell you which path fits the institution and the situation.

How does someone sign from inside?+

Every Florida Department of Corrections institution has staff who can notarize, and most county jails do too. Where that is slow, a mobile notary can be scheduled to visit. Documents go in through legal mail or with the notary, get signed, and come back out. It takes longer than a normal closing because the institution sets the schedule, which is why I say three to ten weeks instead of seven days.

Where does the money go, and who holds it?+

Wherever the owner directs, and never through me. A licensed Florida title company receives the purchase money, pays off the mortgage, taxes and any liens, and sends the balance by wire or check to the person or account the owner names in writing: a family member, an attorney's trust account, the owner's own bank account, or a deposit to the owner's inmate trust account. You will see every number on the closing statement before anything is signed.

Can the state or the prison take the money?+

Sometimes part of it. Court-ordered restitution, fines, and certain liens recorded against the owner or the property get paid at closing like any other lien, and the owner keeps the rest. Florida can also file a lien for the cost of incarceration in some cases. This is why I pull the clerk's records before I give a number: you will know about anything like that before you decide, not at the closing table. If a lien would eat most of the equity, I will tell you so plainly.

What if a spouse or another co-owner is on the deed?+

Everyone on the deed has to sign, and everyone gets the same written offer and the same paperwork. A spouse on the outside signs at the title company or with a mobile notary. The owner inside signs as described above. If the co-owners do not agree, there is no sale, and nobody can be forced.

What if there is a mortgage, back taxes, or code violations?+

That is normal. I buy as-is. The mortgage, delinquent taxes, code fines and liens are paid out of the sale at closing. You do not fix anything, clean anything, or bring money to the table. If the debts are bigger than the value of the house, I will tell you that too.

What does it cost the owner?+

Nothing. No commissions, no fees, no repairs. I pay the standard closing costs, the notary, and the recording. The written offer shows the price and what the owner nets after the mortgage and liens are paid. That number is the number.

How fast?+

A written offer within a few days of our first conversation. Closing usually takes three to ten weeks. Our part of the process moves promptly, but getting documents in and out of an institution runs on the institution's schedule. If anyone promises to close a sale like this in seven days, walk away, and walk fast. No one can make a correctional facility move paperwork that quickly.

Why did my family member get a letter from you?+

Public records. Florida property records are public, and so is the Department of Corrections inmate list. When the same name is on both, I write to the owner, because almost nobody else will. The letter is an option, not an obligation. If the owner is not interested, the reply slip has a box for that, and I will not write again.

What are you not?+

I am not a lawyer, a bondsman, a bail agent, or anyone connected to the owner's case. I cannot give legal advice and I will not pretend to. I am a local buyer who has built a clean way to do this one thing, and I want the owner's family or attorney to look at everything before anyone signs.

What I will put in writing

Anyone can say "fair" on a website. These are the things I commit to on paper, because they are the things people in your position get burned on.
